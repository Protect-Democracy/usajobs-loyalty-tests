11.
A team of analysts presents an evaluation of an enterprise-wide supervisor training program. They ran a standard regression model on a dataset of 50,000 employees across 300 distinct DoD organizations, comparing those whose supervisors took the training against those whose did not. They report a highly significant positive effect on employee retention (p < .001). Assuming they controlled for basic demographics and selection bias, what is the most critical statistical assumption they likely violated by using a standard regression approach on this specific dataset?

 The model violates the assumption of independent observations by ignoring the clustered nature of the data. Employees within the same command share a common environment, which artificially deflates the standard errors in a standard regression, creating a high risk of a Type I error (false positive).
 They failed to report the practical effect size alongside the p-value. With 50,000 data points, almost any minor difference will be statistically significant, but the actual impact of the training might be operationally meaningless.
 They likely used listwise deletion for missing data across such a large dataset, which introduces bias and reduces the generalizability of the findings.
 Standard regression models cannot handle categorical predictor variables like "training status" (Yes/No); they should have used a t-test or ANOVA instead.

A senior executive leader requests an urgent, enterprise-wide survey to be launched in two weeks. They hand you a draft containing 200 questions covering every possible organizational issue. As the lead social scientist, you know a survey of this length imposes an excessive respondent burden, which may lead to high drop-out rates, careless responding, and unusable data. How do you handle this stakeholder?

 Explain the methodological risks of excessive respondent burden to the leader. Suggest compromising by cutting the survey down to the 30 most critical questions and offer to sit down with their staff to help them prioritize the cuts.
 Proceed with launching the survey as requested to maintain the stakeholder relationship but add a technical appendix to the final briefing explaining how the excessive respondent burden and careless responding may have impacted the validity of the data.
 Refuse the request outright, citing DoD statistical policy guidelines on survey burden and OMB/PRA compliance, and inform them that launching a 200-question survey in two weeks is unacceptable.
 Meet with the leader to validate their urgency but pivot the conversation to focus on their core objectives. Facilitate a rapid scoping discussion to identify the top 2-3 strategic decisions they need to make right now. Propose a phased approach: a highly targeted, 15-question pulse survey immediately, followed by qualitative focus groups later to address the secondary issues.

Offer to highlight any minor, secondary positive trends in a separate section while keeping the main finding focused on the overall effect.
