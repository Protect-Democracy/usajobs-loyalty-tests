Assessment 2
You will now complete a Situational Judgement Test (SJT). This assessment presents realistic job-related scenarios. For each one, select the response that best reflects how you would handle the situation. Each question has four response options. These range from the most appropriate to the least appropriate action, but the options are presented in random order. Your task is to choose the option you believe is the best course of action in a federal workplace setting. There are no right or wrong answers. Your responses should reflect sound judgment and professional decision-making. This test evaluates how you approach workplace situations, not your prior experience or background. Please answer honestly and thoughtfully. Your responses will help determine your qualifications for this position.


1.
You are reviewing a Project Management Plan for a new military housing facility in the California desert when you discover that the environmental impact assessment appears to conflict with recent EPA's updated Section 404 guidelines for ephemeral streams, common to desert ecosystems. The project is scheduled to begin construction in six weeks, and the customer has already committed significant resources. What is your most appropriate course of action?

 Contact the environmental coordinator informally to clarify the discrepancy while continuing the administrative and financial review sections of the PMP pending further guidance.
 Approve the PMP as submitted, assuming the original assessment's certification is sufficient and any discrepancies can be addressed through change orders during construction.
 Pause the formal review and convene a technical team including environmental specialists, legal counsel, and project stakeholders to analyze the compliance gap, define mitigation strategies, and revise the PMP before approval.
 Include the compliance issue in your review comments and forward the PMP to the customer, recommending they consult their environmental specialists before construction begins.
 Document the compliance issue in detail and escalate it to your Division Chief, recommending a pause on construction until environmental requirements are verified and the PMP is updated.


2.
You are developing a Project Management Plan (PMP) for a complex facility with a congressionally mandated funding deadline. The Project Delivery Team's (PDT) technical lead reports an 18-month design estimate, but the customer's representative on the PDT is advocating for a 12-month schedule. As the Program Manager, how do you best align the PMP to optimize project parameters and address this discrepancy?

 Agree to the 12-month customer timeline and direct the team to add contractor support, noting the significant schedule and quality risks in the PMP's risk register without proposing specific mitigation measures or alternative approaches.
 Convene the Project Delivery Team (PDT) to conduct a risk analysis on the compressed schedule, develop viable alternatives with phased options and parallel work streams, and present these options to the customer for a collaborative decision.
 Accept the 12-month timeline by directing the team to curtail design reviews and safety assessments, asserting that congressional funding deadlines supersede established USACE quality and safety protocols in the Project Management Plan (PMP).
 Propose a compromise 15-month schedule by overlapping non-critical design activities and formally present this single alternative to the customer along with a high-level overview of the associated risks and potential cost impacts.
 Engage the engineering team to identify critical path activities that can be fast-tracked with added resources, while formally documenting the associated risks to quality and safety standards for inclusion in the Project Management Plan (PMP).


3.
As the Program Manager, you are reviewing a final draft PMP when you identify two critical compliance issues: the acquisition strategy contradicts the signed customer agreement on small business goals, and the proposed acquisition schedule violates federal procurement law. The Project Manager has requested immediate approval to maintain the project's timeline. What is your most appropriate response?

 Withhold approval and schedule an urgent meeting with the project manager and contracting officer to develop a corrective action plan that resolves both the stakeholder agreement and the regulatory compliance conflicts within the PMP.
 Contact the customer directly to renegotiate the small business agreement to align with the current PMP acquisition strategy, while documenting the federal procurement regulation conflicts for resolution during the next project phase review.
 Grant conditional PMP approval, stipulating that the project manager must formally resolve the small business and procurement regulation conflicts within 30 days, or the authority to proceed will be rescinded by the program manager.
 Approve the PMP as submitted, justifying that compliance issues can be addressed through contract modifications during project execution and that maintaining the schedule is the highest priority for successful project delivery.
 Do not approve the PMP. Return it to the project manager with documented findings, directing that the acquisition strategy and schedule be revised to meet all legal and customer agreement requirements before resubmission for a final compliance review.


4.
During a monthly review of a military medical clinic project, you note the Cost Performance Index (CPI) is 0.85 at the 60% completion point, driven by unforeseen soil conditions requiring significant foundation changes. The project manager reports the Schedule Performance Index (SPI) is 1.0 and that the original completion date is not at risk. As the Program Manager, what is your most appropriate response?

 Continue with the current approach, accepting that budget overruns are common risks in construction projects, and plan to address the financial shortfall toward the end of the project if and when it becomes necessary to do so.
 Task the project manager with documenting the cost variance and its root cause, and then coordinate with resource management to explore the use of existing management reserve or contingency funds to cover the projected cost overrun.
 Direct the project manager to absorb the cost overrun by reducing the technical specifications for non-critical project features, thereby maintaining the original budget and the current project completion schedule without formal change requests.
 Direct the project manager to develop a detailed cost recovery plan, identifying potential value engineering savings in non-critical work, while ensuring no compromise to the project's quality or safety standards and documenting all findings.
 Assemble the Project Delivery Team, including cost engineering and contracting, to analyze the cost variance, evaluate alternatives like scope adjustments or additional funding, and prepare a formal change request with detailed justification for the Change Control Board.

 Schedule a meeting with the involved project managers to discuss the root causes of the mischarging, then coordinate with the resource management office to issue revised guidance on proper labor cost allocation and monitoring procedures for future work.

 Issue a directive to all project managers to immediately cease the improper charging, partner with resource management to quantify the total fiscal impact, and develop a comprehensive plan to correct the misaligned cost data in the financial system.
 Allow the mischarging to continue through the end of the fiscal year, justifying that correcting it now would create unacceptable budget variances in the planning accounts and disrupt project execution activities for the remainder of the fiscal year.
 Instruct the project managers to cease the practice going forward, but decide against correcting past data due to the administrative burden, and plan to address the known data inaccuracies during the next annual budget formulation cycle.

 Make an executive decision to prioritize the Environmental team's requirements based on their regulatory nature, and direct the Real Estate team to revise their strategy accordingly to maintain the project's original schedule and customer commitments.

 Authorize the Engineering team to proceed with their design based on current assumptions, justifying that resolving the Environmental and Real Estate conflicts can be managed through contract modifications or change orders after the design is substantially complete.
 Formally task the Environmental and Real Estate leads with resolving their conflict independently and reporting back with a joint solution within two business days, while advising the Engineering team to be prepared for potential design changes.

Concurrently, Resource Management has pending budget actions and Office of Counsel is reviewing permits. How do you restore alignment across these competing functional demands?

 Maintain the current plan without formal intervention, operating under the assumption that each functional organization is solely responsible for its own deliverables and that any integration conflicts can be resolved reactively by the teams as issues arise.
 Personally engage with each functional lead to document their constraints and timelines, then develop and brief a revised, sequenced schedule that de-conflicts the critical path while adhering to all legal and regulatory requirements.
 Authorize Construction to proceed with limited site preparation activities based on preliminary environmental assessments, while individually working with Contracting and Resource Management to expedite their separate supporting tasks and resolve delays as they occur.
 Establish a recurring PDT integration meeting, formalize an Integrated Master Schedule (IMS) to track all dependencies, and update the Programmatic Risk Management Plan with contingency actions that allow for parallel, compliant work where feasible.
 Prioritize efforts on resolving the critical path contracting delays by expediting actions with Contracting and Office of Counsel, while coordinating with Resource Management and Construction to maintain parallel progress on non-dependent activities where possible.

 Document a multi-phased engagement plan: first, meet with each group to define their interests; then, facilitate a collaborative, multi-stakeholder workshop to identify common ground and forge a consensus path forward to inform the final project design.
 Prioritize external communications by holding a public information session to advocate for the project's benefits and address environmental concerns, while tasking the technical leads with resolving the inter-agency design conflicts separately from the public outreach effort.
 Recommend proceeding with the customer's preferred design immediately, asserting that their project authority is primary and that all other stakeholder concerns can be managed reactively through the formal, legally-required public comment period later in the process.
 Propose a plan to systematically interview each stakeholder group to document their requirements and concerns, then have the PDT analyze this data and develop a proposed solution that attempts to address each stakeholder issue individually.

You are preparing for a Program Review with your Division Chief, who needs a briefing package for tomorrow's command-level IPR. Your program faces a 20% cost overrun, critical permit delays, and congressional inquiries. The Chief needs a full picture and defensible recommendations. How do you structure the briefing content?

 Design the briefing to highlight positive program accomplishments first to build confidence, then downplay the challenges as routine, manageable issues that are being handled at the team level and do not require command-level visibility or intervention.
 Structure a briefing with a clear 'bottom line up front' (BLUF), detailing each issue's root cause, program impact, and a specific, resourced action plan with firm recommendations for command-level decisions to maintain program objectives.
 Organize the briefing by issue severity, providing detailed background on each challenge and presenting potential courses of action (COAs) with associated pros, cons, and resource impacts for leadership to deliberate and decide upon.
 Focus the briefing on the 20% cost overrun as the most critical issue, provide a detailed problem statement with context, present your single recommended course of action, and briefly note the other program challenges.
 Prepare a status-focused briefing that lists each program issue and the initial steps the team has taken to address them, then conclude by asking leadership for guidance on how to prioritize these competing challenges.

 Strengthen your existing presentation by adding a detailed risk assessment for non-funding and developing backup slides that specifically address the likely reasons for the previous denial while demonstrating clear mission impact and project criticality.

 Continue to use the existing generic standards without modification, arguing that organizational consistency is paramount and that any differences in project complexity are best addressed through informal, day-to-day feedback and coaching from supervisors.

 Develop a multi-tiered competency model where performance standards directly align with project complexity and PM certification levels, integrating both quantitative metrics (e.g., CPI/SPI) and qualitative assessments of leadership and stakeholder management skills.
 Establish a hybrid performance plan that defines universal competencies for all PMs (e.g., ethics, communication) and adds specialized, weighted evaluation criteria based on whether a project is classified as new construction or routine maintenance.
