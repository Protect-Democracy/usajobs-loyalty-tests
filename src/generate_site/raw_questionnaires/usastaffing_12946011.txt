2.
What is EEOC Management Directive (MD) 110 and how does it guide investigations?

 EEOC MD 110 establishes substantive discrimination law and serves as the primary authority for determining rather discrimination occurs in federal EEO cases
 EEOC MD 110 provides policy guidance and procedural standards for processing federal sector EEO complaints, including investigation requirements, timeliness, affidavits, reports of investigation and investigator neutrality
 EEOC MD 110 provides general guidance on EEO complaint process, primarily focused on agency decision making and legal standards, rather than detailed investigative proceedings
 EEOC MD 110 provides procedural guidance for federal sector EEO complaints, including investigative requirements and report preparation, but does not address timeliness or standards for affidavits


3.
What is the difference between disparate treatment and disparate impact?

 Disparate treatment applies to private sector employees and disparate impact applies to only federal employees
 Disparate treatment focuses on statistical patterns and group outcomes, and disparate impact focuses on an individual’s intent and motive
 Disparate treatment involves intentional discrimination (on a protected basis) and disparate impact is a facially neutral policy that disproportionately affects a protected group and is not justified by business necessity
 Disparate treatment occurs only when there is a written discriminatory policy while disparate impact occurs only when there is verbal harassment


4.
How do claims differ from incidents and evidence?

 Claims are allegations of discrimination accepted for investigation; incidents are the specific factual evidence that give rise to those claims; and evidence consists of testimony or documentation to substantiate or refute the evidence.
 Claims are the conclusions reached by the investigator; incidents are management actions; and evidence is limited to sworn affidavits
 Claims describe the alleged discriminatory action; incidents are the dates and locations where those actions occurred; and evidence includes witness statements and documents related to the claims
 Claims and incidents are interchangeable terms referring to alleged discriminatory events, while evidence is any information collected during the investigation


5.
What makes an affidavit legally sufficient?

 An affidavit is legally sufficient when it is signed and sworn, generally relevant to the complaint, and provides the witnesses’ explanation of events, even if some of the responses are conclusory
 An affidavit is legally sufficient as long as it is included in the ROI and supports one party’s position in the complaint
 An affidavit is legally sufficient when it is sworn or affirmed, relevant to the accepted claims, responsive to the investigative questions, fact based rather than conclusory and addresses elements of the claim
 An affidavit is legally sufficient if it is signed by the witness, addresses the main allegations, and reflects the witnesses’ perspective, regardless of rather all questions are fully answered.

8.
An EEO complaint was referred to ADR, but the matter was not resolved. After ADR concludes, the Complainant tells you that during the mediation the management official admitted the action "was probably handled poorly". How should you as the Investigator handle this information?

 Disregard the ADR communication entirely, do not include it in the investigative record, and proceed with the investigation based only on evidence obtained outside of ADR
 Include the alleged admission in the ROI since it is relevant to the Complainant’s claims
 Use Complainant’s statement to frame follow-up questions to management about rather the actions were mishandled
 Note generally in the ROI that ADR was unsuccessful and that statements were made during mediation that raised concern about management actions


9.
During EEO counseling, a Complainant states they feel pressured to participate in ADR because they were told it would "look bad" if they decline. What is the MOST appropriate response by you as the Investigator?

 Advise the Complainant that ADR is generally preferred by the agency and is the best way to resolve most EEO matters
 Explain that ADR is voluntary, clarify the differences between ADR and the investigative process, ensure the Complainant understands their rights, and document the Complainant’s informed decision.
 Encourage participation in ADR by explaining that it often results in faster resolution and fewer disputes, while noting that participation is technically optional
 Direct the Complainant to participate in ADR to avoid delays in the EEO process
