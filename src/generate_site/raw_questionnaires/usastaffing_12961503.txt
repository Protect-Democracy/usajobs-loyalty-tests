SCENARIO
You are the lead case agent on a complex bribery investigation targeting a senior DHS contracting official. Three months into the case, a cooperating source provides information suggesting a second, more senior DHS official may be accepting payments from the same contractor network. The new information is credible but uncorroborated. Your current case plan is well-developed, and introducing a second subject would significantly expand scope and complexity. Your ASAC is aware of the original investigation but has not yet been briefed on the new information.

What is your most appropriate course of action?
 
 Immediately pivot the investigation to focus on the more senior official, since higher-level targets typically carry greater public interest and prosecutorial value, and notify your ASAC once the new investigative direction is established.
 Document the new source information in your case file, continue the original investigation without modification, and flag the second official's potential involvement in your final report for future investigative consideration.
 Brief your ASAC on the new information promptly, document the source's account in detail, conduct a preliminary assessment to evaluate the corroboration available, and recommend to the ASAC whether the investigation should be expanded, bifurcated, or referred — allowing leadership to make an informed scope decision before any investigative action is taken on the new subject.
 Independently begin developing the new subject through open-source research and database queries to build enough of an evidentiary foundation to make a scope recommendation to your ASAC, then brief leadership once you have something concrete to present.
 Contact the U.S. Attorney's Office to advise them of the potential second subject and ask for prosecutorial guidance before briefing your ASAC, since the expanded scope may affect the prosecution strategy for the original case.

SCENARIO
You are executing a court-authorized search warrant at the residence of a DHS employee suspected of embezzlement. During the search, you discover a locked safe that was not listed in the warrant's specific items to be seized. Your team also observes, in plain view on a desk, printed documents bearing what appear to be classification markings consistent with Secret-level information - items that are also outside the warrant's scope.

How do you proceed with both findings?
 
 Seize both the safe and the classified-appearing documents immediately under the plain view doctrine, given the apparent national security implications of the documents and the likelihood that the safe contains additional relevant evidence.
 Photograph the classified-appearing documents in place without seizing them, secure the area around the safe, halt the search, contact your ASAC and legal counsel immediately, and seek guidance on the appropriate legal basis — including whether a supplemental warrant or national security referral is needed — before taking any further action on either item.
 Seize the documents under plain view given the apparent national security concern, leave the safe in place pending a supplemental warrant application, and document both findings thoroughly in your case notes before continuing the search within the original warrant's scope.
 Continue the authorized search, document the location of both the safe and the documents in detailed notes, and consult with your ASAC and legal counsel upon return to the office to determine the appropriate next steps for both items.
 Immediately halt the search in the areas where the out-of-scope items were found, photograph them in place, secure the premises, and contact your ASAC and legal counsel in real time — before proceeding further — to get legal guidance on both the classified documents and the safe, while continuing the authorized search only in areas unaffected by the out-of-scope discoveries.

SCENARIO
You receive a complaint alleging that a high-ranking DHS official directed subordinates to falsify performance records to shield a poorly performing program from congressional scrutiny. The complaint comes from a single anonymous source who claims to have witnessed the directive. There is no corroborating documentation, no named witnesses, and the official has no prior misconduct history. A similar allegation against the same official was made two years ago and closed without substantiation.


What is the most analytically sound initial approach?
 Treat the prior unsubstantiated allegation as a significant credibility indicator and open a full criminal investigation immediately, since repeated allegations against the same official suggest a pattern that warrants active investigative pursuit.
 Conduct a structured preliminary assessment: evaluate the specificity and internal consistency of the allegation, identify what corroborating evidence could theoretically exist and where, assess whether congressional records or audit findings are available that could independently test the allegation, and document your analysis before briefing the ASAC with a recommended course of action.
 Review the case file from the prior allegation to identify any overlapping elements, conduct a discreet records check on the program in question, and open a preliminary inquiry if the records check produces any anomalies consistent with the allegation.
 Decline to investigate given the anonymous source, absence of corroborating evidence, and the prior unsubstantiated allegation — which together suggest a low-credibility complaint — and document the closure rationale for the ASAC's review.
 Open the investigation and immediately interview the official directly to obtain their account before conducting any records review, preserving the element of surprise while the source's account is still fresh.

SCENARIO
You are six months into a complex procurement fraud investigation. Your analysis has produced substantial circumstantial evidence against the primary subject, but no direct evidence of criminal intent. A cooperating witness has been reliable throughout the investigation. The witness now tells you they are willing to make a recorded statement implicating the subject in exchange for a promise of leniency - a promise you do not have authority to make. The AUSA assigned to the case has not yet been briefed on this development.


What is your most appropriate course of action?

 Decline the witness's offer for now, document it in the case file, and make no further commitments until you have briefed the AUSA and received guidance on whether and how to proceed with a formal cooperation arrangement.
 Advise the witness informally that while you cannot promise leniency, a proffer agreement is something the AUSA's office may consider, and encourage the witness to retain counsel before making any further statements — then immediately brief your ASAC and the AUSA on the development.
 Obtain the recorded statement immediately, since the evidentiary value of direct witness testimony outweighs the procedural concern, and address the leniency question with the AUSA afterward once the recording is secured.
 Immediately brief your ASAC and the AUSA on the witness's offer without making any commitment or taking any further action with the witness, allowing the prosecutorial team to determine the appropriate response — including whether to pursue a formal proffer or cooperation agreement — before re-engaging with the witness.
 Decline the recorded statement to avoid creating a legal challenge around the leniency issue, continue developing the circumstantial evidence already in hand, and revisit whether witness cooperation is needed once the evidentiary record is more fully developed.

 Approach the foreign national directly and identify yourself as a federal law enforcement officer, since the national security implications of the contact justify an immediate investigative response outside the original authorization.

Your investigation timeline has the grand jury term expiring in 60 days.


What is your most appropriate approach to this challenge?
 Accept the 45-day timeline and proprietary format, contact your digital forensics unit immediately to ensure the software capability will be in place before the records arrive, and brief the AUSA on the timeline so they can assess whether a grand jury extension is warranted given the production schedule.
 Brief your ASAC and the AUSA on the production timeline and format constraints immediately, work with the AUSA to determine whether a motion to compel earlier production or a different format is warranted, coordinate with your digital forensics unit on the software requirement, and jointly assess with the AUSA whether a grand jury extension is needed — developing contingency plans for each scenario in parallel.
 Instruct the platform's legal team that the proprietary format is unacceptable and demand production in a standard format within 30 days, citing the grand jury deadline as grounds for an expedited and more accessible production.
 Notify the AUSA of the timeline and format issue, recommend they seek a grand jury extension as the most straightforward solution, and proceed with other investigative workstreams while awaiting production.
 Seek an emergency court order compelling production in a standard format within 15 days, arguing that the 45-day timeline and proprietary format together constitute a de facto obstruction of the grand jury process.

SCENARIO
You have completed a 16-month investigation into a senior DHS official for bribery and conflicts of interest. The evidence strongly supports three of the five original charges but is insufficient for the remaining two. Your investigative report is due to the SAC in five days. Your partner agent has drafted the report in a way that characterizes all five allegations as supported, softening the language around the two weaker charges rather than clearly distinguishing the evidentiary basis for each.


What is your most appropriate course of action?
 Submit your partner's draft as written, since the soft language on the weaker charges leaves room for the SAC and AUSA to make their own evidentiary assessments and avoids creating unnecessary conflict with your partner before the report is submitted.
 Revise the report independently so that each charge is evaluated separately and the evidentiary basis — or its absence — is stated clearly and accurately for each, then discuss the revisions with your partner before submission to the SAC, ensuring the final product reflects the actual state of the evidence.
 Add a separate summary section to your partner's draft that accurately characterizes the evidentiary strength of each charge individually, without modifying their draft narrative, and flag the discrepancy to the SAC when you submit.
 Bring the characterization discrepancy to your ASAC's attention before submission, explain the specific evidentiary distinction between the supported and unsupported charges, and request guidance on how the report should be structured — then revise accordingly before the SAC receives it.
 Rewrite the report so that each charge is assessed separately with clear, evidence-based language; the supported charges are stated as substantiated with specific evidentiary citations; and the two insufficient charges are explicitly identified as unsubstantiated with an explanation of what evidence was sought but not found — then discuss the revisions with your partner and submit the revised product to the ASAC for review before it reaches the SAC.

How do you approach preparing the briefing?

 Provide the IG with a comprehensive briefing that includes all investigative details, since the IG's decision-making authority requires full situational awareness and the IG's office is subject to the same classification and sensitivity protections as the investigation itself.

SCENARIO
You are preparing to present a complex, multi-year bribery case to an AUSA for prosecutorial consideration. The investigation involves a senior DHS official, multiple co-conspirators, and evidence gathered through a combination of electronic surveillance, cooperating witnesses, and financial records analysis. The AUSA is unfamiliar with DHS programs and has limited experience with public corruption cases. You have 90 minutes for the presentation.


How do you structure and deliver the most effective prosecutorial presentation?

 Present the evidence chronologically, walking through each investigative step in sequence, to give the AUSA a complete picture of how the case developed and ensure the evidentiary record is thoroughly documented in the meeting record.
 Open with a brief factual summary of the DHS program context relevant to the corruption scheme, present the case organized by charge rather than by investigative chronology, link each piece of evidence explicitly to the elements of the offense it supports, address the cooperating witness's credibility proactively, and reserve time for the AUSA's questions — bringing a leave-behind summary document the AUSA can reference after the meeting.
 Structure the presentation around the strongest evidence first to build the AUSA's confidence in the case early, then address the more complex or circumstantial elements, and conclude with a clear statement of recommended charges and your assessment of prosecutorial viability.
 Open with a concise executive summary of the charge theory and the key evidence supporting each element, provide sufficient DHS program context for the AUSA to understand the scheme, present the evidence charge-by-charge with explicit legal element mapping, address anticipated defense arguments and evidentiary vulnerabilities proactively, and deliver a clear prosecutorial recommendation — leaving time for substantive AUSA questions and providing a written case summary for follow-up.
 Ask the AUSA what format they prefer before the meeting and tailor your presentation to their stated preferences, since prosecutorial decision-making is most effective when the AUSA feels they are driving the review process rather than receiving a formal briefing.

SCENARIO
You are called to testify before a federal grand jury in a case you led investigating embezzlement by a DHS program manager. During testimony, the defense-aligned subject's personal attorney - who is not present in the grand jury room - has apparently coached the target witness to provide a materially different account from what they told you during voluntary interviews. The grand jury foreperson asks you directly whether the witness's grand jury account is consistent with prior statements they made to you.


What is your most appropriate response?

 Decline to answer the foreperson's question directly, and suggest the question be redirected to the AUSA, since characterizing witness consistency is a legal conclusion that should be made by the prosecutorial team rather than the investigating agent.
 State clearly and accurately that the witness's grand jury account is inconsistent with prior voluntary statements, specify the nature and significance of the inconsistency based on your recollection and documented notes, and provide factual testimony about what the witness previously told you — without editorializing about their credibility or motivations.
 Request a brief recess to consult with the AUSA before answering the foreperson's question, since the evidentiary and legal implications of characterizing prior witness statements in a grand jury setting require prosecutorial coordination before you respond.
 Answer the foreperson's question factually and completely: confirm that the account is inconsistent, describe the specific prior statements the witness made to you with reference to your contemporaneous notes, explain the investigative context in which those statements were obtained, and — if asked — describe the overall significance of the inconsistency to the investigation, relying strictly on documented facts and your direct personal knowledge.
 Characterize the witness as having provided a materially false account, explain to the grand jury that the inconsistency is consistent with coaching by defense counsel, and recommend to the grand jury foreperson that they ask the AUSA to consider whether the witness should be referred for a perjury investigation.
