Social media photos corroborate this, as does the IP address associated with the business drawing down invoices on the award. You are deciding on the first step in managing this complaint. What should be done first?

 Speak to the grants specialist, review the award file, and inquire as to whether the allegation, if proven, would contradict the award terms.
 Obtain travel records for the subject to corroborate their physical location, and coordinate interest in the matter with a Federal prosecutor.
 Intercept the subject during a transition through the U.S. and conduct a consensual subject interview.
 Image the computer and request government e-mail records for the grants specialist to determine whether they are colluding with the funding recipient.


3.
While speaking with the grants specialist, you become aware of a potentially covered financial relationship per statute with the subject/grant recipient, leading you to suspect a possible ethics violation. You have not provided a rights advisement up to this point. What should be the next action?

 Elicit further information about the potentially covered financial relationship with the subject.
 Stop the interview, advise speaking to an attorney before resuming the conversation, providing your business card and departing the room immediately.
 Continue the interview and obtain information relevant only to the conduct contained in the original allegation.

4.
You have reviewed evidence and cannot prove a covered financial relationship existed between the grants specialist and the funding recipient. The program office has elected to cease funding the grantee and considers the matter resolved. How should you proceed?

 Suspend the investigation as the program office has administratively resolved the allegations.
 Conduct a subject interview with the grantee virtually or telephonically to assess the validity of the allegations.
 Continue to develop evidence of potential administrative misconduct on the part of the grants specialist for referral to their management.
 Contact the prosecutor assisting with the case to determine a path forward.

You are leading an investigation into allegations that a contractor has been falsifying labor charges and directing subordinates to mischarge time to government contracts. A complaint from an anonymous employee prompted the investigation. Initial records review suggests the activity may span multiple contracts and two fiscal years. You have access to contract files, timekeeping records, and payroll data. Your initial review suggests the alleged conduct may involve both civil and criminal violations. How should you proceed?

 Open a parallel civil referral to the contracting office while continuing the criminal investigation independently.

6.
You identified the subject's personal financial records as a high-value evidence source. Legal counsel advises two options: an administrative subpoena, which can be issued quickly but may trigger Right to Financial Privacy Act reporting to the account holder, or a grand jury subpoena, which is more covert but requires prosecutor buy-in. The subject has not yet been alerted to the investigation. How should you proceed?

 Pursue the administrative subpoena to maintain investigative momentum.
 Consult with your supervisor and/or prosecutor to weigh the evidentiary risk before selecting an avenue.
 Defer obtaining the financial records until other evidence is developed sufficiently to support the stronger subpoena.
 Conduct a subject interview, claiming to possess more fully developed evidence, and entice the subject into a full confession.

A query of the case management system shows that this official has been very active in coordinating matters with leadership. You become aware that the official is scheduled to meet with your leadership towards the end of the week. How should you proceed?

 Obtain the official's ethics file from their Office of General Counsel.
 Have your source make a consensually monitored phone call to the official to ask about the allegations after obtaining proper authorization.

 Contact the career employee and request a voluntary interview, providing necessary rights advisements and suggesting to meet them outside of the building.


9.
You learn from open-source information that your source has filed a personal lawsuit against the named official, arguing that they have taken regulatory action against the source's company. How should you proceed?

10.
You are leading an investigation into allegations that a contractor submitted fraudulent invoices for services not performed. During a planned evidence collection at the contractor facility, you obtain paper invoice files, electronic billing records from a shared network drive, and a personal laptop voluntarily surrendered by the subject. You learn that the shared network drive is accessible to other employees who may have modified the billing records since the investigation began. How should you proceed?

 Implement a litigation hold on the network drive immediately to prevent further modification before collecting the records and contact cyber agents for support.
 Collect the records as-is and document the access history to establish a baseline for authentication challenges later.
 Weigh the risk of further modification against the time required to implement protective measures before proceeding with collection.
 Consider whether the paper invoice files alone are sufficient before deciding whether to pursue the electronic records.


11.
After the laptop has been transferred to the digital forensics lab and examination has begun, you discover that it was logged under the wrong case number. The lab has already documented their receipt and initial findings under the incorrect number. How should you proceed?

 Suspend the examination and re-collect the laptop to establish a clean chain of custody.
 Coordinate with the lab to ensure the error and its correction are documented consistently across all records.
 Continue the examination while you directly correct the evidence log without coordination with the forensic lab, since the laptop itself has not been compromised.
 Ignore the error as clerical and continue processing the evidence without notifying the forensic lab, since the laptop itself has not been compromised.


12.
The laptop examination is complete. It yielded files relevant to the investigation but nothing that directly corroborates or contradicts the paper invoice files and electronic billing records, which are consistent with each other. How should you proceed?

 Evaluate whether the investigation is at a stage where a prosecutive referral could be supported by the two corroborating sources alone.
 Assess what the laptop findings add to or change about the evidentiary picture before determining next steps.
 Consider whether the time and resources already spent on the laptop examination are justified given the findings.
 Determine what the evidence as a whole establishes against the elements of the criminal offense to guide next steps.
