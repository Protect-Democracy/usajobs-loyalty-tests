2.
As a Strategic Training Specialist, you have led an effort to develop a new training module on a specific adversary's decision-making calculus, as described under your "Implements Global Policy Integration" duties. However, a senior leader at a Combatant Command is resistant to incorporating it into their upcoming joint exercise, believing their existing training is sufficient and that the new material will disrupt their schedule.
 You inform the senior leader that this new adversary module is mandated by Joint Staff doctrine and must be included in the exercise. When the leader pushes back on the schedule disruption, you reiterate that the curriculum is set and refuse to alter the format, implying that their command will be non-compliant if they do not participate.
 You listen to the senior leader's concerns about the tight exercise schedule. You defend the material by providing a standard briefing on why the new adversary doctrine is important. To reach a compromise, you offer a simple solution: you agree to cut the training presentation time in half (from two hours to one hour) if they agree to put it on the schedule.
 Anticipating resistance regarding the schedule, you leverage your established network by socializing the new doctrine with the senior leader’s trusted Operations Officer weeks before the formal meeting. During the meeting with the senior leader, you don't "pitch" the training; instead, you ask the leader about their biggest intelligence gaps regarding this specific adversary. When they describe a gap that your module happens to solve, you frame the new training as a custom solution to their stated problem. You invite the leader to co-author how the module is deployed, asking, "How can we implement this in a way that provides the most benefit to your command?
 You recognize that the senior leader is motivated by conducting a successful, fast-paced exercise without administrative friction. Instead of pushing a standalone brief, you persuade them by linking the new adversary doctrine directly to a recent real-world scenario that this specific Combatant Command struggled with. You negotiate a solution: instead of a traditional classroom module that halts the exercise, you offer to provide the training exclusively to the "White Cell" (exercise controllers). The controllers will then weave the new adversary decision-making calculus directly into the exercise injects.


3.
Your role requires you to collect and synthesize observations from joint operations to provide feedback to Joint Force Commanders. You have identified a recurring "organizational responsibility" flaw where a specific Service component is consistently failing to integrate effectively with the joint force. Your recommendation for improvement is sensitive and likely to be met with resistance from that Service's leadership. How do you influence senior leadership to adopt the new module?

 Long before the draft is finalized, you invite senior representatives from the affected Service to participate in the data-synthesis phase of the "lessons learned" process. During collaborative workshops, you guide them using data to identify the integration gap themselves. You ask: “If you were in charge of fixing this joint integration issue, how would your Service lead the way?" You then co-author the recommendation section of the report with them. You ensure the report highlights both the gap and an innovative pilot program to fix it.
 Before finalizing the report, you schedule informal, one-on-one meetings with key leaders from the affected Service. You frame the integration flaw not as an isolated failure, but as a systemic issue stemming from outdated joint doctrine. You persuade them that addressing this issue now will make their Service more successful in future joint operations. You negotiate the final recommendations together, ensuring their feedback is incorporated and focusing the report on actionable solutions rather than past mistakes.
 Recognizing the report is sensitive, you schedule a meeting with a mid-level action officer from the affected Service. You present the draft and offer to tone down some of the language (e.g., changing "failed to integrate" to "experienced integration challenges") if they agree not to protest the report's submission.
 You draft the report using accurate but critical language, naming the Service component and listing the integration errors. When a representative from that Service asks to review the draft, you state that the report must remain "independent and objective." You publish the report and send it directly to the supported Command.

As part of your role to integrate "allied and partner integration," you are representing the Joint Staff on an interagency working group with members from the Department of State and other agencies. The group is developing a strategic plan for a complex contingency operation. Your analysis, based on joint doctrine, indicates that the proposed plan doesn't allocate sufficient resources for military logistics. The other agencies are pushing back, concerned that a larger military footprint will be politically unpalatable. How will you persuade the interagency partners?
 Prior to the working group, you build relationships with key allied and partner planners. You persuade them by reframing the logistical footprint not as a "military burden," but as a shared asset. You demonstrate how military airlift and medical logistics can act as a "dual-use" spine to support whole of government efforts.
 You use basic data to defend your position, presenting charts on fuel and tonnage requirements. Acknowledging their pushback, you attempt a straightforward negotiation: you offer to cut the logistics footprint by 20% to "meet in the middle" and appease other agencies’ concerns.
 You rely on DoW authority and military doctrine. You state that the military cannot operate effectively without the requested logistics footprint and dismiss political concerns as "outside of the military’s lane." You imply that the entire strategic plan could fail if military logistic support requirements aren't met.
 You actively listen to the interagency partners to fully understand their motivation (fear of a politically unpalatable "occupying" optic). You persuade them by validating this concern, then negotiate a structural compromise: you propose keeping the heavy logistics "over the horizon" (e.g., at an afloat staging base) while only maintaining a minimal, low-visibility footprint in-country.

Design the exercise so that military logistical goals and interagency diplomatic goals remain interdependent. Following the exercise, apply lessons learned to write a joint operating procedure to inform ongoing regional partnerships
 Identify interagency points of contact and build a distribution list. Provide participants with existing training schedule, initial scenario overview, and invitations to planning conferences. Track RSVPs and coordinate base access and seating arrangements to ensure the exercise runs smoothly.
 Recognize that civilian agency partners use entirely different terminology and planning cycles. Prevent friction by facilitating a series of collaborative workshops designed to establish a common language and align planning timelines. Work with the State Department and NGOs to understand unique constraints and integrate specific diplomatic and humanitarian objectives into the master scenario events list.
